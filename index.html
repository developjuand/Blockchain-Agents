From theory to real transactions · TKS on-chain study

Two days, one error: 79% of a month’s failed USDC payments on Base

99.7% of 2.9M USDC transfers on Base succeeded on-chain. Two days, Oct 29–30, produced 79% of all failures, and 99.6% of those were “invalid signature”. On Oct 29, all failures came from 20 submitting addresses.

01 · Why this build

From theory to real transactions

After writing my article on how AI agents will pay each other, I wanted to go further than theory and see how the payment rail actually behaves with real transactions.

x402, an emerging standard for agent payments, settles in USDC on Base using a signed authorization (transferWithAuthorization): the payer signs off-chain, and someone else submits it on-chain.

So I measured every such transfer on Base for 30 days and looked at where and how they fail, searching for patterns. However, this covers all of these transfers, not only agent payments.

02 · The question going in
HYPOTHESIS

What I wanted to know

  • How often do these payments fail on-chain?
  • Are failures constant, or concentrated in time?
  • Are they concentrated in a few submitters?
  • What errors cause them?

Hypothesis from the EPFL paper that I wanted to test: the open problem is the gap between verifying a payment and settling it (state can change in between), plus dependence on a few submitters.

What the data says about this is in section 6.

03 · How I measured

Method

I wrote an article about how AI agents could pay each other. Then I wanted to test the idea with real data instead of only theory. This build is inspired by a recent paper from EPFL, "When HTTP 402 Meets the Blockchain" (Wang et al., 2026), which measured failures in x402 payments. I took a simpler route: public on-chain data from Base.

  • Data: I used Dune (DuneSQL) to measure the data with SQL, querying the tables base.transactions and base.traces.
  • Filter: I looked at the last 30 days, between Oct 5 and Nov 3, 2026 (UTC). I picked every call to the USDC contract on Base (0x833589fcd6edb6e08f4c7c32d4f71b54bda02913) with the function selector 0xe3ee160e (transferWithAuthorization).
  • Failure: for me, a transaction only counts as a failure if it reverted on-chain. I decoded the revert reasons from the trace output. If a payment is rejected before it reaches the chain, it is not visible here.
Show the SQL 
SELECT date_trunc('day', block_time) AS day, COUNT(*) AS total,
  SUM(CASE WHEN success THEN 0 ELSE 1 END) AS failed
FROM base.transactions
WHERE "to" = 0x833589fcd6edb6e08f4c7c32d4f71b54bda02913
  AND bytearray_substring(data, 1, 4) = 0xe3ee160e
  AND block_time >= TIMESTAMP '2026-10-05' AND block_time < TIMESTAMP '2026-11-04'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1

05 · The story

What the month looked like

A

A quiet baseline

On normal days, only ~0.065% of these transfers revert on-chain: 1,752 failures over 28 days.

Across the whole month, 8,542 of 2,911,405 transactions failed (0.29%). Two days account for most of that.

B

Then, one incident

Failures jumped around 16:00 UTC on Oct 29, stayed high overnight, and peaked at 00:00 UTC on Oct 30, with 1,049 failures in that hour.

They dropped back after ~02:00 UTC.

C

One error, almost every time

On normal days, invalid signatures were ~5% of failures. During Oct 29–30 they were 99.6%.

Normal-day failures are mostly balance problems, already-used authorizations and expired authorizations. Why the signatures were invalid during the incident is unknown.

D

Twenty addresses, near-identical counts

On Oct 29, all 4,435 failures came from exactly 20 submitting addresses (the wallets that sent the transactions, likely relayers or facilitators, not the payers), each with ~189–253 failures.

Near-equal counts suggest one automated system or one shared cause. Who operates them is not known from this data.

E

Meanwhile, transfers kept rising

Daily transfers rose roughly 4x over the month, from ~63k/day in the first week to ~247k/day in the last 3 days. Distinct payers did not: ~3,200 per day in both periods. The same payers made more transfers each, about 20 each in the first week and about 78 in the last 3 days.

The incident days (amber) were not unusual in volume or in payers: about 2,600 payers per day, inside the month's normal range (2,005 to 4,590).

06 · What it does and doesn't show

Back to the hypothesis

Normal-day failures (balance gone, authorization used, authorization expired: ≈ 74% of normal failures) are consistent with state changing between verification and settlement. That is still a hypothesis.

The Oct 29–30 incident was a different failure type: invalid signatures at ~1,000x the normal daily rate.

What it shows

  • A low, steady baseline of on-chain reverts (~0.065%).
  • One concentrated incident on Oct 29–30 (79% of the month's failures).
  • One error type and 20 submitting addresses behind it on Oct 29.

What it doesn't show

  • Why the signatures were invalid.
  • Who operates the 20 addresses.
  • Failures that never reached the chain.
  • Whether this traffic was AI agents.

Next questions

  • Decode the failed transactions to see whether the payers (not the submitters) are the same.
  • Check whether the incident repeats.
07 · Conclusion

Why I'm still optimistic

Most things worked. 99.7% of 2.9 million transfers went through, and even on the worst day (Oct 29), 96.3% did. On a normal day, only about 1 in 1,500 fails. The bad days were narrow: one error caused almost every failure, and only 20 of the 117 addresses sending transfers on Oct 29 had any. That looks like one specific thing to fix, not a problem with the whole system. And people stayed: the number of payers held steady, and each one sent more transfers afterwards.

For AI agents, these are the details that matter: failures that are rare and easy to see, with all the data public so anyone can check it. Measuring, testing and fixing a rail like this, one problem at a time, could make it more reliable for agents to pay each other. I don't know if this traffic came from agents. The point is the rail they would use.
